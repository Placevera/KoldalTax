A tax extension is more time to file, not more time to pay

By Alek Koldal, EA · October 16, 2026

Every year I meet people who filed an extension in April and thought they were covered until October. They were, but only for the paperwork. The tax itself was still due in April, and the IRS has been adding penalties and interest ever since.

What an extension actually does

An extension (Form 4868 for individuals) moves your filing deadline from April 15 to October 15. It does not move your payment deadline. Whatever you owe for the year is due on April 15, extension or not.

What people often miss

1. Not paying with the extension. If you expect to owe, estimate the amount and pay it when you file the extension. Anything unpaid after April 15 is charged a late payment penalty of 0.5% per month, up to 25%, plus interest.

2. The 90% safe harbor. If you pay at least 90% of your actual tax by April 15 and pay the rest when you file by October 15, the late payment penalty generally won’t apply. You’ll still owe interest on the balance.

3. Missing the extended deadline too. If October 15 passes without a return, the late filing penalty kicks in: 5% of the unpaid tax per month, up to 25%. Together the two penalties can reach 5% per month. Once you’re more than 60 days late, a minimum penalty also applies: a fixed dollar amount or 100% of the tax due, whichever is less.

4. Forgetting estimated taxes. An extension doesn’t change your quarterly estimated payments. The first one for the new year is still due April 15.

5. Business deadlines are earlier. Calendar-year partnerships and S corporations are due March 15, not April 15. Their late filing penalty is a fixed amount per partner or shareholder, per month, for up to 12 months, and it applies even when no tax is owed.

What it costs: a $5,000 example

Say you owe $5,000 and pay nothing by April 15.

  • You filed on time (or extended) but paid late. The late payment penalty is $25 a month, and interest (7% a year as of late 2026) adds roughly $30 a month. Pay in full on October 15 and the extra cost is about $325.
  • You didn’t file or pay. With no return and no extension, the combined penalties run $250 a month from April 15, or $1,250 after five months. The late payment penalty then keeps adding $25 a month until it reaches its own cap. Together the penalties can reach $2,375, plus interest.
  • You filed but never paid. The $25 a month continues until it reaches $1,250, about four years. If the IRS sends a notice of intent to levy, it doubles to $50 a month. Interest keeps running, and the IRS can eventually file a lien or take money from your paycheck or bank account.

These are rough figures. The IRS counts any part of a month as a full month.

If you’re already late

File as soon as you can, even if you can’t pay in full. That stops the larger penalty. Then:

  • Pay what you can to reduce interest.
  • Ask about a payment plan. If you filed on time (including the extension), an approved installment agreement cuts the late payment penalty to 0.25% per month, as long as the IRS hasn’t already sent a levy notice.
  • Check whether you qualify for penalty relief. First-time abatement generally requires a clean record for the past three years, all required returns filed, and the balance paid or on a payment plan. Starting in 2026, the IRS may apply it automatically for 2025 and later tax years, so check your transcripts to see what’s still there. Events outside your control, such as a serious illness, a death in the family, a disaster or destroyed records, may also count as reasonable cause.

The bottom line

File the extension if you need time, but pay your best estimate by April 15. If you’re unsure what you owe, that’s exactly the question to ask before the deadline, not after.
